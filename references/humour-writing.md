# Humour Writing - the entertainment spec (humour-writing.md)

The bar for every blog post this repo writes: **a standup comedy show.** Not "professional with occasional levity" - every sentence earns its place by being entertaining, or it gets rewritten. People don't stop reading because content is wrong; they stop because it's boring. **The death of attention is being boring.**

`/context-layer` builds the member's version of funny; `/blog-post` writes to this spec; `/audit`'s entertainment score (1-10 per page) grades against it.

## The philosophy

- Your competitors' blogs are walls of dutiful information written by nobody for nobody. Being genuinely fun to read is an open lane in almost every local-business niche - the cheapest differentiation that exists.
- Entertainment IS retention IS rankings: dwell time, scroll depth, return visits are behavioral signals. A reader who laughs finishes the page.
- The humour is a delivery vehicle, never a replacement: every joke carries the information WITH it. Funny-but-useless fails the same as accurate-but-boring.

## The craft (the devices, with the pattern)

1. **Specificity is the joke.** "A water heater lasts 8-12 years" is information. "Your water heater is quietly plotting retirement the day it turns 8" is the same information, alive. Weird, concrete detail beats generic exaggeration every time.
2. **Misdirection** - set up the expected, deliver the unexpected: "There are two kinds of plumbers: licensed professionals, and your brother-in-law."
3. **The rule of three** - two straight items, third one breaks: "You'll need a wrench, a bucket, and the emotional readiness to see what's inside your drain."
4. **Analogy from the reader's world** - explain the technical through the domestic: "Hard water treats your pipes the way cholesterol treats arteries, except no doctor will ever ask about it." Analogies TEACH - they don't count as the joke. A paragraph with a vivid analogy and no joke is still a dry paragraph (see the build rule below).
5. **Self-deprecation over customer-deprecation** - the writer/trade is the butt of jokes, never the reader: "We've spent 15 years with our heads under sinks. Our chiropractor loves us."
6. **Callbacks** - land a joke early, tag it again near the end. Readers feel like insiders; it stitches the page together.
7. **Exaggeration anchored in truth** - inflate the FEELING, never the FACTS. Prices, timelines, and claims stay literal (context/proof.md is law); the drama around them can be operatic.
8. **The stakes are real** - the funniest angle is usually the true one: the 2am flood, the DIY disaster, the quote that made someone sit down. Real stories from `context/proof.md` outperform invented bits, always.

## How a joke is built (analogies are not jokes)

The failure mode that produces dry drafts: **the explanatory analogy dressed as a joke.** The comparison does the teaching, and the writer hopes the mapping itself is funny. It never is - it's homework wearing a funny hat. A blunt true sentence isn't a joke either; it's just accurate.

**The build that lands: say the fact straight, then bolt on one gratuitous, absurdly specific extra.** A prop, a confession, a mock ceremony. The joke never carries the information - it's the unnecessary detail that makes the sentence human.

- Fact: the quote is too high. Joke: "daylight robbery with a clipboard." The clipboard isn't a metaphor for anything - it's a ridiculous concrete object that makes the robbery polite.
- Fact: you pay whether it works or not. Joke: "which I believe is also how my gym membership works. I've been twice since 2019. The second time was to cancel, and I panicked and bought a personal training package."

Four shapes, in order of how often to use them:

1. **The absurd prop** - one concrete, slightly ridiculous object attached to a straight fact ("your wallet just flinched hard enough to pull a muscle").
2. **The universal tangent** - a tiny irrational behavior everyone has personally done, attached to the fact as a comparison ("owners keep checking the phone for a week anyway, the way you keep opening a fridge you already know is empty"). This LOOKS like an analogy but isn't - the fridge explains nothing. An analogy maps mechanism; a tangent maps a shared habit, and the laugh is recognition. The test: would 9 out of 10 readers think "I do that"? The bar for this shape, a four-beat build that got an out-loud laugh: "the same math as the last time you watched a 12-minute drywall tutorial, nodded along, felt genuinely capable, realized around minute nine that no amount of money could make you care about joint compound, and called a guy from Craigslist."
3. **The confession that escalates** - the writer as the butt, deadpan, and the humiliation gets one notch worse than the reader expected. Mock ceremony for trivial failures ("a moment of silence") lives here too.
4. **The stacked escalation** - rule of three where the absurdity climbs ("about as useful as a chocolate teapot, a screen door on a submarine, or a plumber who promises to show up 'sometime between 8 and 5'"). Stacking absurd IMAGES is encouraged when each one escalates; stacking machinery (a pun plus a callback plus a quirky detail on one line) reads as effort.

**The joke lives in the subject.** Every proper noun, dated source, and company on the page carries its own comedic material - the reader's existing feelings about it. A stat from Bing gets a Bing joke ("the search engine you've only ever used by accident"). A Google revenue stat gets mock sympathy for Google. A 2015 study gets its age teased. Before reaching for a generic joke, check what's already sitting in the sentence - the specific thing the reader has an opinion about is always funnier than an imported bit, because the reader does half the work.

**The parenthetical - three flavors, two or three per piece at most.** Never explaining the joke, and a button on every line is a tic:

- **Reaction** (after the punchline): the writer hearing his own joke - "(ouch)" · "(straight A's, somehow)" · "(no offence)".
- **Reflection** (after the punchline): the writer's irrational private feeling, deadpan - "(position 68 deserved more, and I'll die believing that)" - or appealing to the reader about a tiny injustice: "(can you believe we live in a world where...)". Usually the strongest of the three.
- **Inclusion** (mid-sentence, BEFORE the punchline): recruiting the reader as an accomplice - "(we've all been there before)".

When originality and instant recognition compete, recognition wins: a stock phrase everyone knows beats a crafted image that costs a half-second of decode.

**The no-thinking test, on every joke:** read it once, at speed. If getting the joke takes a second step of thinking - decoding a reference, working out a mapping, connecting two ideas the sentence didn't connect - it's not a joke, it's a puzzle, and it gets cut or simplified. Simple and instant beats clever every time, and Grade 10 applies to the jokes hardest of all.

**No half-jokes.** Every line either lands a real laugh or plays it completely straight. A wry tone with no punchline reads as a joke that failed - which is worse than no joke. And when you flatten a half-joke, check whether the flat version still earns its place: the joke was often the only reason the sentence existed, and a line that neither informs nor lands gets DELETED, not flattened. Humour commits or it leaves - there's no middle setting.

**The delete test, on every joke:** delete it. If the sentence loses information, it was an analogy doing the teaching - rewrite the fact straight, then re-attach a joke as an extra. If it loses nothing but the fun, it was a joke. Keep it.

**Never reuse the example lines in this file in a member's draft**, even reworded - they're calibration targets, not source material. Every joke gets built fresh from the member's topic and proof.

## This is the DEFAULT, not a preference

**Ships on. Every member, every blog post, until an industry rule or an explicit instruction turns it down.**

Most members are not writers. Extracting their natural style faithfully means faithfully reproducing weak writing, and defaults are what 10,000 sites will actually read like because almost nobody changes one. So the house style leads and the member's flavour is an **overlay**, not the master:

- **Style comes from this file** - the bar, the devices, the pacing.
- **Material comes from them** - their stories, their real jokes, their trade language, their customers' words. This is the part no competitor can copy and it is never defaulted.
- **Their own writing is an INPUT, not the template.** Mine it for genuine phrases and jokes they have actually made. Never take register, sentence length or structure from a Slack message or a short email - those are a different job at a different pitch.
- If they have a clear kind of funny, match it (dry, deadpan, dad-joke, exaggerated) - forcing a deadpan person to write zany reads fake. If they don't have one, **use this file's default rather than flattening to nothing.**

## ⛔ Industry override - check BEFORE writing a single line

Read `context/business.md`. In these categories humour is not risky, it is disqualifying - the reader is in the worst week of their life and a joke ends the relationship:

**Funeral and cremation · personal injury · oncology and serious illness · bankruptcy and debt · addiction and recovery · divorce and family law · criminal defence · end-of-life veterinary · mental health crisis · abuse and trauma services.**

In these, the dial goes to **zero humour** and the retention levers below carry the whole load. Warmth, plain language and certainty replace wit. Say the switch out loud when it happens so the member knows it was deliberate, not a flat draft.

**Adjacent-but-fine:** dentists, vets, law firms doing conveyancing or wills, accountants, clinics. Normal service, dial slightly down. When genuinely unsure, ask - the cost of asking is a sentence, the cost of a joke in a funeral home is the client.

## Retention is the goal. Humour is the best tool, not the only one.

The real rule is **not one line that lets someone leave.** Humour is the strongest lever in most categories and is unavailable in some, so these carry the page when it is off - and strengthen it when it is on:

1. **Open loops.** Raise a question early, answer it later. "There's one thing on this list that voids your warranty. It's number four."
2. **A number that surprises.** Specific, sourced, counter-intuitive. Concrete beats round: 8,412 beats "thousands".
3. **Tension and stakes.** What it costs to get this wrong, in real terms - the 2am flood, the denied claim, the deadline that passed.
4. **Naming the reader's private worry.** The sentence they were afraid to ask out loud. Highest-retention line on most pages, and it works everywhere including grief categories.
5. **Story momentum.** A real person, a real problem, an unresolved ending pulling the reader forward.
6. **A contrarian claim, then the proof.** "Most people are told to replace it. Usually that's wrong."
7. **Specificity itself** - already device 1 below, and it is the one device that is never turned off, in any industry.

## When NOT to joke, even at full dial

- **Inside a proof block.** Numbers and results need to land clean; a joke next to a price reads as hedging.
- **On the reader's crisis moment.** Describe the 2am flood vividly, joke about the plumber's back, never about their panic.
- **In an FAQ or answer-first block** - see the GEO note below.
- **In the CTA.** The last line before the ask is straight, always.

## ⛔ RANKABILITY OUTRANKS HUMOUR. Always. No exceptions.

**If a sentence has a job in getting found - being lifted into an AI Overview, winning a featured snippet, answering the query - it is written straight. Humour never gets to compromise it.**

Order of precedence, and it is not negotiable:

1. **Can it rank and be cited?** Answer-first blocks, FAQ answers, definitions, the opening 40-60 words under every H2, spec tables, anything a retrieval system will lift. These are declarative, self-contained, no setup, no misdirection, no exaggeration. **Nothing funny happens here.**
2. **Is it accurate?** Proof, prices, timelines, claims. Straight.
3. **Is it entertaining?** Everything else - and that is most of the page.

A joke that costs a citation is a bad trade every time. Being cited is what brings the reader who then enjoys the writing; there is no audience to entertain if the page never surfaces.

**The practical version:** write the extractable skeleton straight first, then make the connective tissue funny. Never the other way round, because retrofitting straightness into a joke usually kills both.

## Humour and AI citation (GEO) - layer it, don't choose

No published research tests humour against AI citation directly, so this is mechanism rather than evidence - flag it that way if a member asks. `references/blog-post-retention.md` carries the measured findings behind the layering.

Retrieval systems cite **passages**, not pages. A sentence built on misdirection or exaggeration is a poor extraction candidate: nothing can lift "your water heater is quietly plotting retirement" as a factual answer. Meanwhile the large majority of AI Overview citations come from pages already ranking in the top 10, and entertainment drives the dwell time and return visits that help get them there.

So humour likely helps you get cited by helping you rank, and hurts the specific sentence being extracted. **Both jobs, same page, different layers:**

- **Answer-first blocks, FAQ answers, definitions, spec tables, the first 2-3 sentences under a heading** - straight, declarative, self-contained, no setup required. These are the extraction targets.
- **Everything around them** - full standup. The prose between the extractable bits is where retention is won and where nothing is being lifted verbatim.

A page that is funny throughout AND has clean liftable answers beats a page that is only one of them.

## Calibration (where the article's restraint advice IS right)

- **PG-13, always** - mild profanity fine if it's the member's voice; never more. Punch at problems, prices, and the trade's absurdities - never at customers, competitors by name, or protected groups. Nothing controversial: a plumbing blog has no business doing politics.
- **Universal references** - jokes a 30-year-old and a 60-year-old both get. No niche memes that date the page in six months, and no reference that needs decoding: "someone's childhood memories of Tuscany" is abstract; "scrolling past the writer's life story to find the actual ingredients" is the same joke in plain sight. Grade 10 applies to the jokes too.
- **Where the dial sits per surface:** blog posts = full standup (nearly every line entertains) · service pages = charm, not comedy (proof does the talking, one or two grins) · GBP posts = one good line each · FAQ/quick answers = straight (these get lifted by AI verbatim - keep them clean).
- **Some jokes will miss.** Fine. Cut the ones that miss on re-read; never let fear of missing produce a boring page.

## The test (run on every draft)

Read it aloud. After every paragraph ask: **would a real person keep reading this at 11pm?** Is there at least one line per section someone might screenshot? If a paragraph is pure dutiful information, it's not done - find the angle, the analogy, or the story. Boring is the only unforgivable failure.
